# Vision narrative sequencing rubric

Five elements, scored in the order Andy Raskin states they must appear in
his "5 Elements of a Great Sales Narrative"
(<https://medium.com/the-mission/the-greatest-sales-deck-ive-ever-seen-4f4ef3391ba0>).
The distinguishing signal is not just whether each element appears, but
whether it appears *in order* — a "vision" stated after the product pitch
does not satisfy elements 1–3, no matter how well-worded it is.

Three rules apply to every element.

- **Quote the right speaker.** For what the rep said, quote the rep. For whether it landed, the buyer's reply must show it. A story that only the rep tells, with no reaction, is Partial at most.
- **Absence is a result.** An element that the call never touched is Missed. Order counts, so an element stated after the product pitch does not satisfy elements 1 to 3.
- **Read the "does not count" line.** Each element lists the usual false positives.

## 1. Name a Big, Relevant Change in the World

**What it is.** Whether the rep opens by naming an external shift —
market, regulatory, technological, competitive — rather than starting with
the product or the buyer's internal pain. Raskin: don't kick off with the
product; instead "highlight the undeniable shift in the world that creates
both big stakes and huge urgency for your prospect."

**Covered** requires the change to be named as something happening *in the
world*. **Missed** looks like starting from the buyer's internal pain
instead ("your team's process is slow" is a pain point, not a change) or
skipping straight to the product.

"We're seeing a lot of interest in modernizing" is Missed — no named
shift; "the whole industry's moving from annual underwriting reviews to
continuous, real-time risk scoring" is Covered — a specific, external
change named before anything about the product.

**Does not count:** the buyer's internal pain restated as a trend. A vague "the market is changing". A change that the rep names only after the product pitch.

**A strong call** names one external shift and says why it matters now.

## 2. Show There'll Be Winners and Losers

**What it is.** Whether the rep makes the change's stakes concrete — who
wins by adapting, who loses by not. Raskin frames this as showing "big
winners and big losers": a positive future for those who adapt, an
unacceptably negative future for those who don't.

**Covered** requires both sides named or clearly implied. **Missed** looks
like a vague "the market is changing" with no stakes attached to either
side.

"The market's shifting, for sure" is Missed — no stakes; "carriers who
move now win renewals on better pricing, the ones still on annual reviews
are already facing costlier reinsurance treaties" is Covered — both sides
named with a concrete consequence.

**Does not count:** a stakes statement with only one side. A generic warning with no consequence for this buyer.

**A strong call** names who wins and who loses, with one concrete consequence for each.

## 3. Tease the Promised Land

**What it is.** Whether the rep describes what success looks like *before*
introducing the product. Raskin: this is "a new future state, not your
product or service" — what life looks like after the buyer has already
benefited.

**Covered** requires the promised land described independent of any named
feature. **Partial at best** if the "future state" is really just the
product working — that reads as a product demo, not an outcome.

"You'd be able to run reports faster" ties straight to a feature and is
Partial at best; "eighteen months from now, you're pulling a live risk
score, feeding it to reinsurers in real time, and renewal conversations
start from what actually changed, not a stale annual file" is Covered — an
outcome described before any feature is named.

**Does not count:** the product working, described as the future. A benefit tied to a named feature.

**A strong call** describes life after the change, in the buyer's terms, before any feature is named.

## 4. Introduce Features as "Magic Gifts" for Overcoming Obstacles to the Promised Land

**What it is.** Whether the rep frames product capabilities as tools for
reaching the already-established promised land, rather than as a
capability list on their own merits. Raskin's phrasing: features
positioned "like the lightsaber, wizardry and spells — as magic gifts for
helping your main character (prospect) reach that much-desired Promised
Land."

**Covered** requires each feature tied back to a specific obstacle
standing between the buyer and the promised land already described in
element 3. **Missed** looks like a feature list recited on its own merits,
disconnected from anything named earlier in the call.

"We also have a reporting dashboard, and an API, and SSO" recited as a
list is Missed; "the data unification layer is what closes that gap
between your claims system and underwriting — that's the piece standing
between you and the real-time score we just talked about" is Covered.

**Does not count:** a feature list with no obstacle behind it. A capability that the buyer did not ask about and did not react to.

**A strong call** ties each feature to one obstacle on the road to the promised land.

## 5. Present Evidence that You Can Make the Story Come True

**What it is.** Whether the rep backs the narrative with proof — ideally a
customer success story showing someone else reaching the same promised
land. Raskin: "the most effective type of evidence is a success story
about how you've already helped someone else" achieve the outcome.

**Covered** requires a concrete before/after story. **Partial** looks like
a stat or logo slide with no story behind it.

"We work with a lot of carriers your size" is Partial — no story; "one
mid-size commercial carrier went from six weeks to four days on renewal
prep after switching on the same scoring engine" is Covered — a specific
before/after.

**Does not count:** a customer logo with no result. A claim from a slide that the buyer never engaged with.

**A strong call** gives one proof point of the same kind as the buyer, with a result the buyer reacts to.

## Sequencing violation (the distinctive failure mode)

Score each element's status first, then check order. If element 4
(features) is introduced before elements 1–3 are established — i.e., the
rep pitches product first and only states a "change in the world" or
"promised land" afterward, as a recap or justification — flag this
explicitly as a **sequencing violation**, regardless of how each element
scores in isolation. A call can score Covered on all five elements
individually and still carry a sequencing violation if the order was
wrong; call this out as a separate, named finding, not folded into any one
element's Note.

## Overlap boundary with `challenger`

`challenger` scores whether a call taught the buyer a new insight
(Teach/Tailor/Take-control) and whether the commercial-teaching pitch arc's
"New way" step appeared before "Our solution." This skill is narrower: it
scores only whether a future-state vision — the "change in the world,"
stakes, and promised land — was built before any feature was introduced,
and treats a rep who pitches features first and bolts a vision on
afterward as a distinct, named failure. If a call would score identically
on both skills, that call likely has no sequencing violation and no missed
teaching moment — use `challenger` for the teaching-quality question and
this skill for the sequencing question.

## Reads-well-too test

A call that clearly builds vision before product across all five elements
should not come back with a manufactured sequencing violation just because
the rep circled back to reinforce the vision after introducing a feature —
that's reinforcement, not a violation. A violation requires elements 1–3
being genuinely absent *before* element 4 first appears, not merely
revisited after. If a call plainly led with vision, don't force a finding.
