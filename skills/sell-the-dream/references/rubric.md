# Vision narrative sequencing rubric

Five elements, scored in the order Andy Raskin states they must appear in
his "5 Elements of a Great Sales Narrative"
(<https://medium.com/the-mission/the-greatest-sales-deck-ive-ever-seen-4f4ef3391ba0>).
The distinguishing signal is not just whether each element appears, but
whether it appears *in order* — a "vision" stated after the product pitch
does not satisfy elements 1–3, no matter how well-worded it is.

## 1. Name a Big, Relevant Change in the World

Does the rep open by naming an external shift — market, regulatory,
technological, competitive — rather than starting with the product or the
buyer's internal pain? Raskin: don't kick off with the product; instead
"highlight the undeniable shift in the world that creates both big stakes
and huge urgency for your prospect." Covered requires the change to be
named as something happening *in the world*, not "your team's process is
slow" (that's an internal pain point, not a change).

## 2. Show There'll Be Winners and Losers

Does the rep make the change's stakes concrete — who wins by adapting, who
loses by not? Raskin frames this as showing "big winners and big losers":
a positive future for those who adapt, an unacceptably negative future for
those who don't. Covered requires both sides named or clearly implied, not
just a vague "the market is changing" with no stakes attached.

## 3. Tease the Promised Land

Does the rep describe what success looks like *before* introducing the
product? Raskin: this is "a new future state, not your product or
service" — what life looks like after the buyer has already benefited.
Covered requires the promised land to be described independent of any
named feature or capability — if the "future state" is just a description
of the product working, that's Partial at best; it should read as an
outcome, not a product demo.

## 4. Introduce Features as "Magic Gifts" for Overcoming Obstacles to the Promised Land

Does the rep frame product capabilities as tools for reaching the already-
established promised land, rather than as a capability list on their own
merits? Raskin's phrasing: features positioned "like the lightsaber,
wizardry and spells — as magic gifts for helping your main character
(prospect) reach that much-desired Promised Land." Covered requires each
feature introduced to be tied back to a specific obstacle standing between
the buyer and the promised land already described in element 3.

## 5. Present Evidence that You Can Make the Story Come True

Does the rep back the narrative with proof — ideally a customer success
story showing someone else reaching the same promised land? Raskin: "the
most effective type of evidence is a success story about how you've
already helped someone else" achieve the outcome. A stat or logo slide
without a concrete before/after story is Partial.

## Sequencing violation (the distinctive failure mode)

Score each element's status first, then check order. If element 4
(features) is introduced before elements 1–3 are established — i.e., the
rep pitches product first and only states a "change in the world" or
"promised land" afterward, as a recap or justification — flag this
explicitly as a **sequencing violation**, regardless of how each element
scores in isolation. A call can score Covered on all five elements
individually and still carry a sequencing violation if the order was
wrong; call this out as a separate, named finding, not folded into any one
element's Note.

## Overlap boundary with `challenger`

`challenger` scores whether a call taught the buyer a new insight
(Teach/Tailor/Take-control) and whether the commercial-teaching pitch arc's
"New way" step appeared before "Our solution." This skill is narrower: it
scores only whether a future-state vision — the "change in the world,"
stakes, and promised land — was built before any feature was introduced,
and treats a rep who pitches features first and bolts a vision on
afterward as a distinct, named failure. If a call would score identically
on both skills, that call likely has no sequencing violation and no missed
teaching moment — use `challenger` for the teaching-quality question and
this skill for the sequencing question.
