# Discovery-call rubric

This rubric has nine dimensions of what a thorough B2B discovery call covers. Each entry describes the dimension. It is not a script. A call can cover a dimension in any order and in any words, and still score as Covered.

Three rules apply to every dimension.

- **The buyer must say it.** Evidence is something the buyer said. A claim from the rep does not count. A fact the rep reads out of the CRM does not count either, unless the buyer agrees on the call. A job title is not authority. A friendly tone is not interest.
- **Absence is a result.** A call can leave a dimension untouched. Then the status is Missed. Say so plainly. Do not credit a dimension because the rep seemed prepared. Do not guess from context.
- **Read the "does not count" line.** Each entry lists the usual false positives. Read that line before you write Covered or Partial.

## 1. Pain & business impact

**What it is.** The rep got the buyer to describe a specific problem in their own words. The rep then pushed past the first vague answer to a business impact. The impact is a cost, a time, a risk, or a number the buyer volunteered.

**Covered:** a concrete problem tied to a quantified impact. The buyer described it unprompted or returned to it more than once. **Partial:** a real problem named once, with no cause and no number. **Missed:** only generic "we are always looking to improve" language, with no specific owned problem.

**Does not count:** a problem that the rep names and the buyer never raised. A complaint about a vendor the buyer uses today. That complaint is an objection or a current-state detail. A pain also needs to exist without any vendor in the room.

**A strong call** asks for the last real example of the problem. It then asks where the number comes from and who tracks it.

"We have some inefficiency in that process" is a Partial at best. "That costs us about 10 hours a week across the team" is Covered.

## 2. Current state & tooling

**What it is.** What the buyer does today to handle this problem. It can be a competitor tool, a manual process, a spreadsheet, or nothing at all.

**Covered:** the current tool or process is named specifically, with enough detail to compare against. **Partial:** a current approach is named in general terms ("we use spreadsheets") with no workflow detail. **Missed:** the call never sets a baseline. A solution cannot credibly claim to improve on the unknown.

**Does not count:** a tool that the rep assumes the buyer uses. A tool mentioned in passing that has no link to this problem.

**A strong call** walks the buyer through how the last case went, step by step, before the rep names any solution.

## 3. Trigger: why now

**What it is.** Why this buyer is looking at a solution this quarter and not last year or next year. The cause is an event, a mandate, or a change that raised urgency. General curiosity is not a trigger.

**Covered:** a specific event, deadline, or change is named and tied to the decision to act now. **Partial:** the buyer asserts urgency ("we need this soon") with no triggering event. **Missed:** no reason appears for why now and not any other time. This is often the earliest sign that a healthy-looking deal will stall.

**Does not count:** an event mentioned only while the buyer explains something else. Urgency that the rep supplies ("I imagine this is a priority?") and the buyer only accepts.

**A strong call** asks what changed. It also asks what the cost is of no change by a stated date.

## 4. Desired outcome & success criteria

**What it is.** What the buyer calls "solved". The best answer is something they can measure later. "Make things better" is not an outcome. A specific, recognizable win is one.

**Covered:** a measurable target, or a description specific enough to test later. **Partial:** an outcome is described but stays qualitative ("just need it to be easier"). **Missed:** no outcome is ever stated beyond interest in the category.

**Does not count:** adjectives with no measure ("faster", "better"). The rep's own list of benefits.

**A strong call** asks the buyer to define success as a measure, a target, and a date.

## 5. Decision process & stakeholders

**What it is.** Who else takes part in the decision: the champion, the economic buyer, and other approvers. It also covers whether the rep mapped this beyond the person on the call.

**Covered:** the economic buyer or other approvers are named, with some sense of the approval order. **Partial:** another stakeholder is mentioned vaguely ("my team needs to weigh in") and not named. **Missed:** the call ends and nobody knows whether the person on the call can say yes.

**Does not count:** a job title alone. The buyer must say who has the final word. A champion is defined by action, such as setting up meetings or taking the deal upstairs. Friendliness and seniority do not make a champion. A person whom others wait on for sign-off is the decision maker, not the champion.

**A strong call** maps who recommends, who approves, and who must be consulted, by name and role. It asks whether the approver will join the next call.

## 6. Budget & timeline

**What it is.** The question is not "what is your budget" as one blunt ask. The question is about signal. Did the call show what this problem is worth? Did it show the date a decision must land?

**Covered:** a figure, a range, or an approval status, plus a specific decision date or window. **Partial:** one of the two surfaces. Either a budget signal with no date, or a date with no budget signal. **Missed:** neither surfaces. "Sometime this year, not sure on cost" is the most specific thing said.

**Does not count:** worry about price on its own. A date for the next meeting. A hypothetical, such as "how long does an evaluation take?". "Maybe next quarter".

**A strong call** asks what fixes the decision date and what a slip costs. It then tests the date against the gates the buyer named.

## 7. Procurement & buying path

**What it is.** This matters for any deal beyond a very small one. The rep needs a sense of the buyer's procurement process, such as security review, legal, and vendor onboarding. The rep learns it early so it is not a late surprise.

**Covered:** a specific procurement step is named, such as a security review or a named approval body. **Partial:** procurement is acknowledged but not detailed. **Missed:** it never comes up. Suppose the deal is so small that these steps probably do not apply. Then say that in the note and do not mark the dimension Covered.

**Does not count:** a generic "we need to talk to legal" with no step named. That is Partial at most.

**A strong call** asks who runs each step and how long each step took last time. It places those steps on the timeline.

## 8. Competitive landscape

**What it is.** Whether the buyer is evaluating anything else, formally or informally. It also covers whether the rep learned this without a blunt "who else are you talking to" that lands as adversarial.

**Covered:** a specific alternative surfaces, such as a named competitor or "doing nothing", with some sense of how the buyer views it. **Partial:** the buyer mentions evaluating options and names none. **Missed:** competition never comes up, although the deal is contestable.

**Does not count:** an evaluation that ended long ago, unless the buyer says what it taught them. "Other options" with nothing behind it is Partial.

**A strong call** asks which option the buyer prefers today and what gap that option has. It then turns that gap into a yes-or-no test.

## 9. Rapport & engagement

**What it is.** Whether this was a genuine two-way conversation. In a good call the buyer opens up beyond transactional answers and adds context or research of their own. In a weak call the rep works through a script and the buyer gives short answers.

**Covered:** the buyer volunteers something beyond what was asked, such as context, a story, or a reaction. **Partial:** the buyer answers only after a prompt, with minimal and generic detail. **Missed:** the call goes straight to business, the answers stay short and closed, and the rep dominates the talk time. Low engagement here is often the earliest signal that a deal is less live than it looks on paper.

**Does not count:** politeness. Small talk that only the rep makes.

## Reads-well-too test

Run this test in both directions before you finalize an audit.

- A call that you know covered discovery well must not come back with several Missed dimensions. When it does, the scoring is biased toward finding fault. Widen what counts as evidence.
- A call that never raised a dimension must not get credit for it. When a status rests on a line from a "does not count" list, remove that status.
