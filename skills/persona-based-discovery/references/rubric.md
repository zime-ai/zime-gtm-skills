# Persona-adaptation rubric

Five dimensions of whether a discovery call was tailored to the specific
buyer persona(s) in the room, not run as a one-size-fits-all script. Score
each persona present on the call separately where the call had more than
one — a call can nail this for the economic buyer and miss it entirely for
the technical evaluator on the same line.

Three rules apply to every dimension.

- **Quote the right speaker.** For what the rep did, quote the rep. For a persona's role, concern, or reaction, the buyer must say it. A job title alone does not set a persona's authority.
- **Absence is a result.** A dimension that the call never touched is Missed. Dimension 1 gates the rest, so when the call never identifies the persona, do not credit dimensions 2 to 5 for adaptation that is not there.
- **Read the "does not count" line.** Each dimension lists the usual false positives.

## 1. Persona correctly identified

**What it is.** Whether the rep established, early in the call, who they
were actually talking to — role, function, day-to-day responsibilities, and
what that person is actually on the hook for — rather than assuming it from
the meeting title or a LinkedIn glance. This also covers whether the rep
picked up on how seriously this person is evaluating: someone running a
live initiative reads very differently from someone comparing options out
of curiosity with no real plan to switch.

**Covered looks like:** the rep asks a direct question about role and
responsibility and gets a real answer back, and the call's later behavior
shows the rep registered whether this person is actively driving a
decision or just gathering information. **Partial looks like:** a role is
named but the rep never confirms what that person actually owns or how
seriously they're evaluating. **Missed looks like:** the call never
establishes this and just pitches, regardless of how good the pitch was.

"Walk me through your role and what you're responsible for here" landing a
real answer, followed by the rep treating the rest of the call
accordingly, is Covered; the rep addressing the room generically without
ever asking is Missed.

**Does not count:** a role the rep guessed from the meeting title. A buyer's enthusiasm, which says nothing about what that person owns.

**A strong call** asks the persona what they own and how they will judge success.

## 2. Pain framed in that persona's language

**What it is.** Once the persona is known, whether the rep framed the
problem in terms that persona actually cares about, or fell back to
generic pitch language regardless of who was listening.

**Covered looks like:** the same underlying problem gets translated
differently for different personas — technical cost for one, business cost
for another. **Partial looks like:** the framing is roughly in the right
direction but stays generic enough it could apply to anyone. **Missed
looks like:** the identical pitch language is reused verbatim across two
different personas on the same call, which is itself a signal of a script
rather than adaptation.

A technical evaluator hearing "this will reduce your integration overhead
and on-call load" is Covered; the same rep telling an economic buyer that
exact sentence instead of translating it to cost or risk is Missed for
that persona.

**Does not count:** one generic benefit line repeated to every persona. Technical words aimed at a business buyer.

**A strong call** restates the same problem in each persona's own terms: cost and risk for the economic buyer, effort and security for the evaluator.

## 3. Persona-appropriate proof point offered

**What it is.** Whether the rep backed the pitch with the kind of proof
that persona would find credible — technical depth (architecture, API,
security posture) for an evaluator, ROI or business-case numbers for an
economic buyer, day-to-day workflow or time-saved for an end user.

**Covered looks like:** the proof point matches both the persona's role
and the specific question they raised. **Partial looks like:** the right
category of proof was offered but aimed at the wrong altitude — too
general for someone asking a specific technical question, or too granular
for someone who asked a business-level question. **Missed looks like:** the
proof point is aimed at a different persona entirely.

Offering an ROI deck to a technical evaluator who asked how the API
handles auth, or a webhook-latency benchmark to an economic buyer who
asked about payback period, is a Missed or Partial depending on how far off
it lands — the proof point exists but was aimed at the wrong audience.

**Does not count:** a case study from an unrelated industry. A proof point that nobody asked for.

**A strong call** shows proof at the altitude of the question that the persona asked.

## 4. Persona-specific objections surfaced and addressed

**What it is.** Whether the rep drew out and directly addressed the
hesitation that persona specifically would have — an evaluator's concern
about implementation lift or security review, an economic buyer's concern
about payback period or switching cost, an end user's concern about
workflow disruption.

**Covered looks like:** the persona voices a specific concern and the rep
answers it directly. **Partial looks like:** a concern surfaces but the
rep's answer stays general rather than addressing what was actually asked.
**Missed looks like:** an objection that plainly belongs to this persona's
role never comes up at all, or the rep changes the subject rather than
answering it. A call where no objection surfaces from either side may mean
there genuinely were none, or may mean the rep didn't probe — mark
**Unclear** rather than assuming either.

**Does not count:** a concern that only the rep names. A complaint about a vendor the persona uses today, which is a current-state detail.

**A strong call** asks each persona what would stop them from saying yes.

## 5. Next step framed for that persona to act on

**What it is.** Whether the proposed next step is something this specific
persona can actually say yes to and move — a technical trial or sandbox
access for an evaluator, a business-case document or exec review for an
economic buyer, a workflow walkthrough for an end user.

**Covered looks like:** the next step is named specifically and matches
what this persona has the authority and interest to act on. **Partial
looks like:** a next step exists but is generic enough it doesn't clearly
map to this persona's role. **Missed looks like:** the same "let's set up
a follow-up call" is offered to every persona in the room regardless of
role.

**Does not count:** "let us set up a follow-up call" offered to everyone. A next step that the persona has no authority to approve.

**A strong call** gives each persona a next step they can act on alone, with a date.

## Reads-well-too test

Before finalizing an audit, sanity-check it against a call you already know
adapted well to its persona(s). If the rubric still returns several Missed
dimensions on a genuinely well-adapted call, the read is biased toward
finding fault — widen what counts as evidence before reporting. Also check
the reverse: a call that never identifies personas at all but scores well
on dimensions 2-5 anyway is a sign the read is inferring adaptation that
wasn't actually there — dimension 1 gates the rest.
