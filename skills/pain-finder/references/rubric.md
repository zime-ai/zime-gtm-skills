# Pain-finder rubric

Used for every pain-finder run.

## Five generic buckets

1. **Operational friction** — manual work, broken workflows, tooling gaps,
   things that cost time or introduce error today.
2. **Risk exposure** — what could go wrong, has gone wrong, or nearly went
   wrong if the current state continues.
3. **Cost / headcount pressure** — budget constraints, understaffing,
   inability to get resources approved.
4. **Growth / scale pressure** — the current approach worked at a smaller
   size and is visibly not working now.
5. **Leadership / stakeholder pressure** — a board, executive, or external
   party (auditor, regulator, customer) is pushing for change from above.

A pain point can span more than one bucket — say so rather than forcing a
single tag.

## Confidence calibration

**What it is.** How strongly the prospect's own words back this pain as
something real and worth solving now — not how compelling the category
sounds, and not how many buckets it touches. A pain only counts at all if
the prospect acknowledged it as real and relevant in their own words, not a
challenge the rep raised or assumed.

**High looks like:** urgency and business impact both present — a specific
incident, a number, or a cost the prospect names unprompted, and the
prospect returns to it more than once. A reference to a prior attempt that
already failed to fix it is a strong High signal; it shows the pain has
outlasted at least one fix already.

**Medium looks like:** clearly stated once, with real specificity, but not
dwelt on — noticeable frustration or disruption that would keep growing if
left alone, without the repetition or quantification that would push it to
High.

**Low looks like:** raised only when asked directly, described in passing
with no incident or number attached, or explicitly downplayed by the
prospect in the same breath ("that's not really what's keeping me up at
night"). Report Low pains — don't drop them — but rank them last and never
let the closing Notes section lead with one.

"We've got some inefficiency in that process" is Low-to-Medium on its own;
"that's the second time this month scheduling has double-booked a crew,
and we already tried fixing it with a shared calendar that didn't hold" is
High — specific, repeated, and shows a failed prior attempt.

## What does not count as a pain

Read this list before you list a pain point.

- A problem that the rep names and the buyer never raised.
- A complaint about a vendor the buyer uses today. It is an objection or a current-state detail. A real pain still exists with no vendor in the room.
- A generic industry problem with no sign that this buyer feels it.
- A roadmap item, or a feature the buyer wants, shown as a current problem.
- "Nothing found" written up as a pain. Absence of a pain is a gap in the call and belongs in Notes.

## A strong call probes in order

First ask about the pain. Then ask how long it has gone on. Then ask who feels it most. Each follow-up narrows the same thread. A pain whose probes ended at the first answer stays Low, however strong the first answer sounds.

## Hard rules

- Every pain point needs a direct quote. No quote, no pain point — an
  inferred read belongs in Notes, explicitly labeled as inferred.
- If the prospect explicitly downplays something, the write-up must reflect
  that downplaying, not silently upgrade it because it fits a compelling
  category.
- Don't invent a "next steps for your follow-up" section unless asked. This
  skill's job stops at ranking pain, not prescribing a sales motion.
